# Story Builder

Turn whatever the user gives you into a complete story that actually works, by making sure every essential building block is present and doing its job before any prose is written.

The diagram `assets/story-building-blocks.png` shows the whole model at a glance. Read `references/building-blocks.md` for the full definition of each block, the questions that draw it out, and how it typically fails. Read `references/worked-example.md` when you want to see a thin idea taken all the way to a finished blueprint. The example only demonstrates the method. Never borrow its characters, setting, or plot, because the user should get a story that grows from their own input, not a variation on the example.

## Why this order matters

Most weak stories fail structurally, not at the sentence level: the hero wants nothing specific, nothing stands in the way, or the ending happens *to* the hero instead of *because of* them. Polishing prose cannot fix that. So build the skeleton first, test it, then write. The blocks should be invisible in the finished story, the way bones are invisible in a body.

## The building blocks

| # | Block | Its job |
|---|-------|---------|
| 1 | Protagonist | The person we follow, with a flaw or gap they may not see |
| 2 | Inciting incident | The event that breaks normal life and forces a response |
| 3 | Desire, external (want) | The concrete, visible goal the plot chases |
| 3 | Desire, internal (need) | What the protagonist truly lacks; carries the meaning |
| - | Antagonist | Whatever blocks the want: a person, group, nature, time, or the self |
| 4 | Journey | Pursuing the goal in unfamiliar territory, with escalating obstacles |
| 5 | Crisis | The darkest moment and the hardest choice |
| 6 | Climax | The protagonist acts on that choice; want and need collide |
| 7 | Resolution | The new normal; proof of change |

## Workflow

### Step 1: Map what the user gave you

Read the input and sort it into the blocks. For each block, note whether it is **given** (stated), **implied** (clearly suggested), or **missing**. Users usually supply one or two blocks, most often a protagonist or a situation. Respect everything they gave: their details are the point, so build around them instead of replacing them.

### Step 2: Fill the gaps with strong choices

Decide whether to ask or invent:

- **Invent by default.** If the user asked for a story, they want a story, not a questionnaire. Make bold, specific choices that grow out of what they gave you.
- **Ask first only if** the user signals they want to co-create ("help me develop", "let's work on"), or if one missing piece would change everything and you genuinely can't infer it (for example, whether this is for children or adults). Then ask at most two short questions and offer a suggested answer for each so they can just say yes.

When inventing, derive blocks from each other rather than picking them independently:
- Derive the **internal need** from the protagonist's flaw. A lonely character needs connection; a controlling one needs to trust; a coward needs courage.
- Choose an **inciting incident** that strikes exactly at that flaw.
- Pick an **external want** whose pursuit will force the protagonist to confront the need.
- Design an **antagonist** that is strong enough to make the want genuinely hard, and ideally one that exploits the flaw.
- Build a **crisis** as a real dilemma: two goods, or two evils, where choosing costs something.
- Make the **climax** the protagonist's own action, and the moment the need is met (or tragically refused).
- Let the **resolution** mirror the opening so the change is visible.

### Step 3: Write the blueprint and the one-sentence spine

Fill in the template below. Then compress it into a single "Once upon a time" sentence. If you cannot write that sentence cleanly, the structure is not ready yet; go back and fix the weak block.

```
STORY BLUEPRINT
Protagonist:        [who, plus the flaw or gap]
Internal need:      [what they truly lack]
Inciting incident:  [the event that breaks normal life]
External want:      [the concrete goal it creates]
Antagonist:         [what stands in the way, and why it's strong]
Journey:            [3-5 escalating obstacles or turns]
Crisis:             [darkest moment + the choice they face]
Climax:             [the decisive act; how want and need collide]
Resolution:         [the new normal; how we see the change]

Spine: Once upon a time, [protagonist] ... [inciting incident] ... wanting [want] ...
against [antagonist] ... until [crisis] ... so [climax] ... and [resolution].
```

### Step 4: Stress-test before writing

Check the blueprint against these questions. Fix anything that fails; these are the most common reasons a story falls flat.

1. Could you film the want? A goal like "be happy" is not a want; "win back the bakery" is.
2. Does chasing the want force the protagonist to face the need?
3. Is the antagonist strong enough that the outcome is genuinely in doubt?
4. Does each journey obstacle raise the stakes over the last, rather than repeat it?
5. Is the crisis a real choice with a real cost, not just bad luck?
6. Does the protagonist cause the climax, rather than being rescued or saved by coincidence?
7. Does the resolution show the protagonist changed (or tragically unchanged), compared with page one?

### Step 5: Write the story

Default proportions, adjustable to the requested length:

| Section | Share of length |
|---|---|
| Protagonist and normal world, flaw on display | ~10% |
| Inciting incident and the want taking shape | ~10% |
| Journey, with escalating obstacles | ~45% |
| Crisis | ~10-15% |
| Climax | ~15% |
| Resolution | ~5-10% |

Writing guidance:
- Default length is roughly 1,500-2,500 words unless the user specifies length, format, or audience. Match the format they ask for (short story, bedtime story, screenplay scenes, comic script, outline).
- Introduce the protagonist through an action that reveals the flaw, not through a description of it.
- Get to the inciting incident quickly. Readers lose interest in "normal life" that goes on too long.
- Dramatize in scenes with dialogue and concrete sensory detail; summarize only the connective tissue.
- Never name the blocks in the prose ("this was his crisis"). The structure should be felt, not announced.
- Let the climax be the most vivid, slowest-motion passage in the piece.
- End on an image that echoes the opening, so the reader can feel the change.

### Step 6: Deliver

Output, in this order:
1. **Title**
2. **The story**
3. **Story map**: a compact table showing how each building block landed, plus the one-sentence spine. This lets the user see the structure and revise it.

Skip the story map if the user just wants the story (for example a bedtime story to read aloud) or says they don't want the breakdown. If the user only asked for structure or a plot, deliver the blueprint and spine without writing the full story, and offer to write it.

## Revising an existing draft

If the user gives you their own draft, don't rewrite it from scratch. Map the draft onto the blocks, name the one or two weakest blocks with a concrete reason, propose specific fixes, then revise only what's needed, keeping the user's voice.

# The Building Blocks in Depth

The model follows John Yorke's account in *Into the Woods* of the essential components shared by almost every story. Definitions here are written for practical use when building a story.

## 1. Protagonist
**What it is:** The character whose desire drives the story and whose change gives it meaning. Usually a person, but can be an animal, object, or group, as long as it wants something.
**Draw it out:** Who is this? What do they do on an ordinary day? What is wrong with their life that they haven't fixed or don't see?
**Common failures:** A passive protagonist things happen to; a protagonist with no flaw (nothing to change); a protagonist we're told about but never see acting.

## 2. Desire: external want and internal need
**External want:** A concrete, visible goal. Getting it, or failing to, should be something you could film.
**Internal need:** The deeper lack, often unconscious. It is what the protagonist must learn, accept, or become.
**The key relationship:** The want drives the plot; the need drives the meaning. The strongest stories make them pull against each other, so achieving one costs the other, or the protagonist can only get the want by finally meeting the need.
**Draw it out:** What do they think will fix their life? What would actually fix it?
**Common failures:** A vague want ("to be happy"); a need that has nothing to do with the plot; a want that appears halfway through instead of right after the inciting incident.

## 3. Antagonist
**What it is:** Whatever stands between the protagonist and the want. It can be a villain, a rival, an institution, society, nature, time pressure, or the protagonist's own flaw. Many stories layer several.
**Why it matters:** The story is only as strong as its opposition. A weak antagonist means no doubt about the outcome, and no tension.
**Draw it out:** Who or what loses if the protagonist wins? What does the antagonist want? How does it exploit the protagonist's weakness?
**Common failures:** An antagonist that appears only at the end; one that's defeated too easily; pure evil with no understandable logic.

## 4. Inciting incident
**What it is:** The event that disrupts normal life and makes the status quo impossible to keep. It creates the want.
**Draw it out:** What happens that the protagonist can't ignore? Why does it hit them in particular?
**Common failures:** Arriving too late; being something the protagonist could simply walk away from; being unrelated to the flaw.

## 5. Journey
**What it is:** The pursuit of the want into unfamiliar territory, "into the woods". The protagonist tries approaches that don't fully work, meets allies and enemies, and is changed in small ways along the way.
**Shape:** Escalation. Each obstacle should be harder or more costly than the last. A useful pattern is try, fail, learn, try harder, with a midpoint turn where the protagonist gains something important (or learns the true nature of the problem).
**Common failures:** Repetitive obstacles; obstacles solved by luck; a sagging middle where nothing changes.

## 6. Crisis
**What it is:** The darkest point. Everything the protagonist tried has failed, the antagonist appears to have won, and they face a dilemma: a choice between two goods or two evils, where either option costs something real.
**Draw it out:** What is the worst thing that could happen to them, related to their flaw? What choice reveals who they really are?
**Common failures:** Bad luck instead of a choice; a choice with an obviously right answer and no cost.

## 7. Climax
**What it is:** The final confrontation where the protagonist acts on the crisis choice. It answers the story's central question: do they get the want? It also settles the need: have they changed?
**Draw it out:** What is the bravest, hardest thing they could do here? How does it require the lesson of the need?
**Common failures:** Rescue by someone else; a coincidence; a fight that has nothing to do with the inner change.

## 8. Resolution
**What it is:** The aftermath. A new normal that shows the cost and the reward of the climax, and makes the change visible, ideally by echoing an image or situation from the opening.
**Common failures:** Ending too abruptly to feel the change; dragging on with explanation; tying up every thread too neatly.

## A note on tragedy
In a tragic shape, the protagonist refuses the need at the crisis, and the climax and resolution show the cost of that refusal. Every block is still present; the arc simply bends the other way.
